A wellness franchise cost worksheet: look beyond the initial fee

Organise startup costs, recurring obligations, delivery costs, and cash timing before evaluating a wellness franchise investment.

What belongs in a wellness franchise cost worksheet?

Separate the initial investment from recurring operating costs and the cash needed while the business develops. Record the source, timing, and uncertainty of each estimate. Then review the model with qualified advisers using the current franchise documents and local quotes.

The initial fee is a visible starting point. A decision also depends on premises, people, delivery, ongoing obligations, and the timing of payments. A cost worksheet helps turn a general ownership conversation into questions you can investigate.

This is a planning framework, not a Kivi investment estimate, earnings representation, or personalised investment recommendation. No example here supplies Kivi fees, expected sales, profit, or a suggested amount to invest.

How should you organise startup and recurring costs?

Give each expense a category, an amount supported by a source, a payment date, and an owner responsible for confirming it. Separate deposits and upfront purchases from monthly obligations. Mark unknown items rather than quietly treating them as zero.

These are categories to investigate, not a statement that every listed cost applies to every franchise. Use the actual agreement to identify mandatory purchases, fees, and services.

The FTC’s franchise buying guide explains that ownership may involve startup expenses, ongoing royalties, advertising fees, and contractual controls. It recommends examining the disclosure document and investigating the opportunity rather than relying on promotional claims.

What is the difference between revenue, operating profit, and available cash?

Revenue is the value of sales recognised under the accounting approach used. Operating profit reflects operating revenue and expenses under defined accounting rules. Available cash depends on when money is actually received and paid. A worksheet should not use those terms interchangeably.

Work with your accountant to define each measure consistently. Upfront equipment payments, deposits, borrowing, tax payments, and customer payment timing can affect cash differently from an operating-profit calculation. Owner compensation and financing costs also need explicit treatment in the model.

Keep a simple question next to each line: “When would this money leave or enter the bank account?” Then compare that cash schedule with the operating model. A forecast that appears positive on paper may still require cash before customer receipts arrive.

How can a break-even calculation help—and where does it stop?

A simplified break-even calculation asks how many units of a defined service would cover the costs included in the model. It depends on assumptions about price, variable costs, fixed costs, and service mix. It does not predict demand or establish that the business will reach that sales level.

The U.S. Small Business Administration’s break-even calculator uses fixed costs divided by the difference between unit price and variable cost per unit. Its definitions distinguish time-based fixed costs from costs that change with volume.

For a service business, define the unit carefully and use the same time period throughout. If there are several offers, changing staff requirements, discounts, or mixed fixed-and-variable expenses, a single-unit calculation may be too simple. Ask an adviser to help model the actual mix and any staffing thresholds.

Use the result to ask operational questions: Does the assumed volume fit the premises, qualified staff, opening hours, and delivery capacity? What evidence supports demand at that price? Arithmetic alone cannot answer those questions.

Which assumptions should investors challenge before proceeding?

Review a slower launch, higher delivery costs, and additional staffing needs alongside the initial case. Identify which assumptions have evidence and which remain guesses. A useful model shows how decisions depend on uncertain inputs rather than hiding that uncertainty in one attractive total.

  • Launch timing: What costs continue if opening is delayed?
  • Demand: What local evidence supports the proposed audience and offer?
  • Staffing: Who delivers and manages the service, including cover for absence?
  • Customer continuity: What evidence supports assumptions about repeat purchases or retention?
  • Contract obligations: Which payments continue regardless of sales?
  • Owner role: What work and compensation have you included for yourself?

Start the Kivi conversation through the investor pathway and request current information for the specific opportunity. Pair the worksheet with the owner-role planning guide if you are also balancing professional work. The franchise evaluation guide covers the broader service and support questions.

Frequently asked questions

Does this worksheet show how much a Kivi franchise costs?

No. Request current investment requirements, fees, and applicable documents for the specific opportunity, then verify local costs.

Does reaching operating break-even mean the original investment is recovered?

No. Covering the operating costs included in a model is different from recovering startup investment, servicing finance, paying taxes, or meeting the owner’s financial goals.

Can I use sales figures as a proxy for owner income?

No. Costs, financing, taxes, payment timing, and owner compensation all affect the relationship between sales and the owner’s financial position.

Does a franchise guarantee a return?

No. Franchise ownership involves business risk. Evaluate the specific documents, assumptions, resources, and operating responsibilities with qualified advisers.
